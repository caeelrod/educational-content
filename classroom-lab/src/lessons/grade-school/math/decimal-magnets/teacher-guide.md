# Teacher guide: Decimal Magnets

**Status:** Demo — not approved for classroom use yet.

## Outcomes
Students multiply and divide whole numbers and decimals by 10, 100, 1,000, and 10,000 by moving a decimal point. They add placeholder zeros where needed and explain the move with place value: multiplying by 100 makes every digit worth 100 times as much, so each digit shifts two places to the left. On the board, that looks like the point moving two places to the right.

## Before class
- Try one turn of each kind yourself: a whole number such as 450 ÷ 100, a decimal such as 4.072 × 1,000, and a small decimal such as 3.5 ÷ 100 (needs a 0 in the ones place and a placeholder zero).
- Open **Teacher settings** and choose the starting level. A gentle start is calls up to 100, multiply only, 2 to 3 digits.
- On a projector, the board, call card, and **Next student** button are drawn large for projection, but check them from the back of your room. On an interactive whiteboard, students can drag the magnet with a finger.
- Settings and the score reset when you change a setting. Nothing about students is saved.

## Facilitation steps
**Whole class line-up (the original routine)**
1. Choose **Whole class line-up**. Students line up as they would at the real board.
2. Read the call aloud (or press the speaker button). If **Predict bigger or smaller** is on, the student at the front answers first.
3. The student drags or taps the magnet, then presses **Check**. The class can call out the hop count as the student moves.
4. If it's wrong, the hint names the mistake. Let the student try again, or ask the class for help before pressing **Show the answer**.
5. Point at the numbered hops and the place-value chart for a few seconds, then press **Next student**. With **Keep the digits** on, the next student starts from the last answer, just like leaving the magnets on the board.
6. Turn on **I'll choose each call myself** if you want to call specific moves, for example to work on dividing.

**Practice on my own**
1. Students work through rounds of 10 on Chromebooks.
2. At the end, they see a score, a list of problems, and a tally of mistake types. **Retry the ones I missed** replays exactly those problems.

**Other options**
- **Commas:** after the point is right, students put the commas back. Commas come off whenever the point moves, so the old commas can't mislead them.
- **Show calls as exponents** (× 10³) or a mix, once students know powers of ten.
- **Mystery calls:** students see the number before and after and name the call. This reverses the thinking and works well as a warm-up.
- **Put your own number on the board** lets you match the digits you'd put up in real life.

## Discussion prompts
- Before moving: will the answer be bigger or smaller? How do you know?
- Why does 1,000 mean three places? What does the exponent in 10³ tell you?
- The point went past the last digit. What goes in the empty places, and why can't we leave them out?
- Is 3.4 × 10 the same as 3.40? Why not?
- Look at the place-value chart. Did the point really move, or did the digits?
- Where do commas go in 4072500? Where do you start counting?

## Misconceptions and how to address them
- **Moving the wrong way:** The prediction step links the operation to "bigger" or "smaller" before the magnet moves. The hint says which way makes a number bigger.
- **Moving the wrong number of places:** The board shows a live "Moved 2 places right" counter. The hint asks students to count the zeros (or read the exponent). The success screen numbers each hop.
- **"Just add a zero":** When a decimal is multiplied, the success screen points out that writing a zero on the end doesn't change the value (3.4 × 10 is 34, not 3.40).
- **Missing placeholder zeros:** Zeros appear in a different style as the point passes the digits, and the explanation says how many were added.
- **Missing 0 in the ones place:** When the answer is less than 1, a lighter 0 appears before the point, and the feedback explains why we write it (0.045, not .045).
- **The point "moves":** The place-value chart shows the point fixed between the ones and tenths while the digits slide. The caption says "The point never really moves."
- **Commas:** The hint tells students to count groups of three starting at the point. A comma placed after the point gets its own message: commas only go in the whole-number part.

## Assessment / expected responses
- 4.072 × 100 = 407.2 (2 places right).
- 450 ÷ 100 = 4.5 (2 places left; the placeholder zero comes off).
- 3.5 ÷ 100 = 0.035 (2 places left; one placeholder zero and a 0 in the ones place).
- 4,072.5 × 1,000 = 4,072,500 (3 places right; two placeholder zeros; commas after the 4 and after the 2).
- 0.06 × 10² = 6.
- Mystery: 52.7 became 0.527, so the call was ÷ 100.
- Strong explanation: "Dividing by 100 makes each digit worth 1/100 as much, so the 5 goes from the tens place to the tenths place. That's two places, so the point moves two places to the left."

## Accommodations and extension ideas
- Support: Start with multiply only and calls up to 10. Leave the prediction step on. Use 2 to 3 digits. Keep a place-value chart on the wall.
- Support: Students who use a keyboard or switch can move the magnet with the arrow keys and press Enter to check.
- Challenge: Calls up to 10,000, exponent form, 4 to 6 digits, and commas on.
- Challenge: Mystery calls, then ask students to write their own mystery for a partner.
- Extension: Chain three calls (× 1,000, ÷ 10, ÷ 10) and ask what single call would have done the same thing.

## Simplifications / limitations
- Numbers are limited to 7 whole-number digits and 4 decimal places, so some calls are skipped for very large or very small numbers.
- Only powers of ten are used.
- "Moving the point" is a shortcut. The place-value chart shows what really happens, and the class discussion should name the difference.
- Scores last only until the page is closed or a setting changes. No student information is collected or stored.
- The read-aloud button depends on the device's built-in speech voices and is hidden when they aren't available.
