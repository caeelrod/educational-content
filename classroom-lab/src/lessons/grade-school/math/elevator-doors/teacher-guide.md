# Teacher guide: Elevator Doors

**Status:** Demo — not approved for classroom use yet.

## Outcomes
Students list every factor of a whole number by testing candidates in order (2, 3, 4, …). Each factor goes on the left door and its partner goes on the right door. Students explain why the search is finished once the doors meet.

## Before class
- Students should be comfortable with multiplication facts and division with remainders.
- Try 24, 36, and 17 yourself. Together they show a typical number, a square number, and a prime number.

## Facilitation steps
1. Project the activity with 24 selected. Explain the setup: 1 always starts on the left door and 24 starts on the right door.
2. Before each click, ask the class to predict whether the next number is a factor.
3. After 2 is placed, point out that the range of possibilities is now 3 to 11, and ask why 13 to 23 no longer need testing.
4. Continue until the doors close. Read the factor list together.
5. Students try 36 and 30 on their own, then a number of their choice.

## Discussion prompts
- After 2 × 12 is placed, why can't any number between 13 and 23 be a factor of 24?
- Why does 6 stand alone in the middle for 36? What other numbers have a middle factor?
- The doors take a long time to close for 17. Is there a way to know sooner that you're done?

## Misconceptions and how to address them
- **Forgetting 1 or the number itself:** The elevator always starts with them on the doors.
- **Finding a factor but missing its partner:** Every factor placed on the left door automatically puts its partner on the right door.
- **Not knowing when to stop:** The shrinking range shows exactly which numbers are still possible.
- **Counting a square number's middle factor twice:** 6 × 6 = 36 places one 6 in the middle, not one on each door.

## Assessment / expected responses
- 24 → 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 24 (8 factors).
- 36 → 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 9, 12, 18, 36 (9 factors; 6 is in the middle).
- 17 → 1, 17 (prime).
- Strong explanation: "Any factor bigger than 12 would need a partner smaller than 2, and the only one is 1, which is already paired with 24."

## Accommodations and extension ideas
- Support: Allow a multiplication chart. Start with small numbers such as 12 or 18.
- Extension (square-root shortcut): For larger numbers, a puzzle appears once k × k is bigger than the number, asking whether the doors can close early. For example, when factoring 97, students can stop after trying 9.
- Extension (common factors): Run the elevator on two numbers (e.g., 24 and 36) and circle the factors they share. This leads into the greatest common factor and simplifying fractions.

## Simplifications / limitations
- Numbers are limited to 2–100.
- Following the method exactly, the doors close only when they meet a factor pair, so prime numbers need many clicks unless students use the shortcut puzzle.
- Only one number at a time. A common-factors mode has not been built yet.
