# Lesson brief: Inside a Human Cell

- Subject / grade: Science / Grade 5 (confirm placement with the teacher; cell structure is often taught in middle school, so this may serve as enrichment or a preview)
- Student learning objective (observable): The student identifies at least eight parts of a typical human cell on a model, states the job of each in their own words, puts the main stops of a protein's journey (nucleus, ribosome, rough ER, Golgi, cell membrane) in order, and explains how one specialized cell's shape or parts fit its job.
- Topic / motivating question: What is inside one of the trillions of cells that make up your body, and how do the parts work together?
- Standards (jurisdiction + version/year + exact code + URL; or TBD): TBD. Verify against the Oklahoma Academic Standards for Science at https://oklahoma.gov/education/services/standards-learning/oklahoma-academic-standards.html with the teacher. Don't assume NGSS codes carry over, and confirm whether cell structure is in the grade 5 standards at all.
- Lesson type: Interactive (browser), four activities in one lesson:
  1. **Explore:** a drawn cell with a zoom lens. Pointing at a part (mouse), tapping it (touch), or choosing it with the arrow keys zooms the lens to that part and shows its job, a longer description, a city analogy, a fact, and how many are in a cell. **Look closer** zooms further into 10 parts, with numbered spots for smaller pieces (for example nuclear pores, cristae, ribosome subunits).
  2. **Protein journey:** a seven-step guided tour that follows insulin from a gene in the DNA, through a ribosome, the ER, and the Golgi, and out of the cell, with an animated traveler and a discussion prompt per step, then a "put the stops in order" check.
  3. **Find the part:** rounds of 8 clues (by job, by name, or by city analogy). Students tap the part. Wrong answers name what they tapped and its job, with targeted hints for common mix-ups (nucleus vs. nucleolus, rough vs. smooth ER, vesicle vs. lysosome). After two misses, **Show me** blinks the answer. The round ends with a score and **Practice the ones I missed**.
  4. **Cell gallery:** six specialized cells (red blood cell, neuron, skeletal muscle, skin, white blood cell, fat cell) with shape, nucleus, a fact, a think-about-it prompt, and a link back to the related part in Explore.
- Target duration: About 30 minutes for all four; each activity also works alone in 5 to 15 minutes.
- Classroom setting: Projector or interactive whiteboard for a whole-class tour, or individual Chromebooks and tablets.
- Prior knowledge: Living things are made of cells; basic idea of food giving energy; breathing in oxygen and out carbon dioxide.
- Key misconceptions:
  - Cells are flat, empty bags with a few parts floating in them.
  - All human cells look the same (the textbook drawing).
  - Every cell has exactly one nucleus (red blood cells have none; skeletal muscle cells have many).
  - Mitochondria "make" energy (they release energy stored in food).
  - Human cells have cell walls or chloroplasts.
  - The DNA itself leaves the nucleus to build proteins (only copies leave).
  - The colors and sizes in the drawing are real.
- Student actions and feedback: Point, tap, or arrow-key to parts; zoom closer and pick sub-parts; step through the protein journey and order its stops; answer clue rounds with mistake-specific feedback; compare specialized cells.
- Formative assessment / evidence of learning: Find-the-part score and missed-parts list (not stored); the journey ordering check; discussion prompts in the journey and gallery.
- Accessibility / device constraints: Hover is never required. Every part can be chosen by tap, by the part buttons, or with arrow keys on the focused cell (Enter looks closer, Escape zooms out). Callouts and all controls are real buttons. Selections are announced in a polite live region. Optional read-aloud uses the browser's built-in speech. Zoom and animations snap instantly under reduced motion. On phones the zoom view sits directly under the cell. Tap targets for tiny parts (ribosomes, vesicles, centrosome, fibers) are enlarged invisibly.
- Reliable educational sources: OpenStax Biology 2e, Chapter 4 (cell structure); NHGRI Talking Glossary; Sender, Fuchs & Milo (2016) for the roughly 30 trillion cells and the share that are red blood cells. All drawings are original SVG. No outside images or question banks were copied.
- Known simplifications and limitations: See `lesson.json` `modelLimitations`. The on-screen notes tell students the drawing is a model, not to scale, and colored only to tell parts apart.
- Teacher sign-off needed: Yes. Confirm grade placement, vocabulary load (some terms such as "cristae" appear only in the optional close-ups), the analogy wording, and the facts listed in the teacher guide.
