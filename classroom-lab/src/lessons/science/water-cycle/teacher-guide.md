# Teacher guide: The Water Cycle

**Status:** Demo — not approved for classroom use yet.

## Outcomes
Students describe evaporation, condensation, precipitation, and collection/movement as processes in a connected, continuous cycle. They trace more than one path a water drop could take.

## Before class
- Students should know that water can be a solid, liquid, or gas, and that heating and cooling can change its state.
- Step through all four stages once. Each stage highlights and animates its part of the picture.

## Facilitation steps
1. Show stage 1. Ask students where the wavy lines are going and what is causing them to rise.
2. Stage 2: Point out the air moving up the hill. Ask what happens to air as it rises (it cools).
3. Stage 3: Ask what has to happen to cloud droplets before they fall.
4. Stage 4: Compare runoff (over the ground) with groundwater (soaking in). Which is faster?
5. Press **Back to evaporation ↻** to show that the cycle continues.
6. Students draw or tell two different paths a single drop could take.

## Discussion prompts
- Does water have to go through every step in the pictured order?
- Where could water stay for a long time? (Oceans, ice, groundwater.)
- The wavy lines show water vapor, but can you actually see water vapor?

## Misconceptions and how to address them
- **The cycle is a fixed loop:** Use the closing note and the "two paths" task. Water can evaporate from a puddle, fall back into the ocean, or stay underground for years.
- **Clouds are water vapor:** Clouds are tiny liquid droplets and/or ice crystals. Water vapor is an invisible gas, which is why the picture uses lines and the note says so.
- **Rain only stays on the surface:** Stage 4 shows water soaking into the ground and becoming groundwater.

## Assessment / expected responses
- Evaporation: the Sun's energy warms water, which turns into vapor and rises.
- Condensation: rising air cools, and vapor turns into droplets that form clouds.
- Precipitation: droplets or ice particles grow large enough to fall.
- Collection: water runs off into streams and the ocean, soaks into the ground, or is used by living things.

## Accommodations and extension ideas
- Support: Pair students and have one narrate the picture while the other reads the text.
- Extension: Add a tree to a printout and ask how water could move through it (transpiration).
- Extension: Ask why a hillside facing the ocean might get more rain than the other side of the hill.

## Simplifications / limitations
- Not drawn to scale. No amounts of water or how long water stays in each place.
- Shows one route. Lakes, ice, snowpack, and transpiration are not drawn.
- Water vapor is drawn as wavy lines even though it is invisible.
- The labels inside the picture are hidden on phone-width screens.
