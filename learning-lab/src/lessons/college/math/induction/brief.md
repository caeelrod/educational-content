# Lesson brief: Proof by Induction

- Section / subject / level: College / Math / Discrete Mathematics, usually the first or second proof technique taught after direct proof and contradiction.
- Student learning objective (observable): The student explains, with the domino model or in words, why a base case plus an inductive step proves P(n) for every n at or above the base; explains why assuming P(k) is not circular; completes an induction proof of a summation identity and of an inequality, marking where the hypothesis is used; and identifies the failing part of a flawed induction proof (missing base case, step that fails for one k, reversed direction, step valid only for larger k).
- Topic / motivating question: How can two finite checks prove infinitely many statements, and why isn't "assume it's true for k" cheating?
- Standards: None. Undergraduate mathematics has no single standards body. Course placement is recorded in `curriculum/college/syllabus-map.md`.
- Lesson type: Interactive (browser), four activities in one lesson:
  1. **Why it works:** a line of dominoes with P(n) = "domino n falls." Students choose which domino the base case pushes and tap gaps to move dominoes apart, predict which fall (all, some, none), then push. A row of P(1), P(2), … chips lights up as dominoes fall, and the feedback ties the outcome to the logic (no base case, one broken link, starting later). An expandable note explains why assuming P(k) is not circular and gives the well-ordering argument in domino language.
  2. **Proof workshop:** three claims (1 + ⋯ + n = n(n+1)/2; sum of the first n odd numbers is n²; 2ⁿ > n). Students check the base case, choose the correct inductive hypothesis from three options, then build the step one line at a time from three choices each. Every wrong choice explains a specific common error (assuming P(k+1), dropping the new term, assuming the goal, mishandling an inequality). A picture beside the proof (staircase, L-shaped layers, doubling bars) highlights exactly what P(k+1) adds to P(k), with a slider for k.
  3. **Tiling puzzle:** the classic theorem that a 2ⁿ × 2ⁿ board missing any one square can be tiled with L-trominoes. Students remove any square, predict where the first L goes in the inductive step, and then reveal the recursion level by level. A note connects the proof to a recursive program.
  4. **Spot the flaw:** six short "proofs," four broken (all horses are the same color; n = n + 1 with no base case; the sum formula proved backwards; 2ⁿ > n² from n = 1) and two correct (sum of odds; 4¢ and 5¢ postage from 12¢). Students click the failing part or say it's valid. Feedback explains the flaw and the fix. The round ends with a first-try score and a review list.
- Target duration: About 45 minutes for all four; each activity works alone in 8 to 15 minutes.
- Setting: Self-study on a laptop or phone, projected in lecture or recitation, or in tutoring.
- Prior knowledge: High-school algebra (factoring, simplifying fractions, exponent rules) and the meaning of an if-then statement. Summation notation is not required; sums are written out with ⋯.
- Key misconceptions: See `lesson.json`. The central one is that assuming P(k) is circular. The lesson addresses it head-on in activity 1 and again through the workshop's distractors.
- Student actions and feedback: Configure and run the dominoes after predicting; choose hypotheses and proof lines with per-choice explanations; predict the tromino placement; locate flaws with per-part feedback.
- Formative assessment / evidence of learning: Domino predictions, number of wrong picks per workshop proof, the tromino prediction, and the Spot the flaw first-try score and review list. Nothing is stored.
- Accessibility / device constraints: All controls are buttons, selects, or sliders with labels. Domino gaps are focusable and toggle with Enter or Space. The tiling board is a single focusable element driven by arrow keys and Enter. Feedback is announced in polite live regions. Domino animation is skipped under reduced motion. Layouts collapse to one column on phones; the domino line scrolls sideways on narrow screens. Math is rendered with Unicode and HTML (no external fonts or math libraries), and choice buttons carry plain-text labels for screen readers.
- Reliable educational sources: Levin, *Discrete Mathematics: An Open Introduction*, Section 2.5 (Induction); Rosen, *Discrete Mathematics and Its Applications*, Chapter 5; Golomb (1954) for the tromino theorem. The flawed proofs are standard classroom examples (the horses paradox is usually credited to George Pólya); their wording here is original. No textbook figures or problem sets were copied.
- Known simplifications and limitations: See `lesson.json` `modelLimitations`.
- Reviewer sign-off needed: Yes. A discrete mathematics instructor should confirm the mathematics, the wording of the hypothesis (some courses prefer "for some k" and others "let k be arbitrary"), and fit with the course's conventions (starting at 0 or 1, notation).
