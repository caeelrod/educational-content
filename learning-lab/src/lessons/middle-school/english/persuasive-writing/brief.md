# Lesson brief: Persuasive Writing

- Section / subject / level: Middle School / English language arts / Grades 6 to 8. Written with Grade 7 in mind, since that is where many standards first ask students to acknowledge opposing claims. Grade 6 classes can skip or slow down part 4; Grade 8 classes can move faster through parts 1 and 2.
- Student learning objective (observable): The student labels the claim, evidence, reasoning, counterclaim, and rebuttal in a persuasive paragraph; sorts statements into arguable claims, facts, personal tastes, and vague statements; picks the strong evidence for a claim and says why the rest is weak; picks reasoning that explains how evidence supports a claim; picks a fair counterclaim and a rebuttal that answers it with a reason; and writes a paragraph that contains all five parts.
- Topic / motivating question: How do you change someone’s mind with writing, without just saying it louder?
- Standards: TBD. None attached. Teachers in Oklahoma should check the Oklahoma Academic Standards for English Language Arts (https://oklahoma.gov/education/services/standards-learning/oklahoma-academic-standards.html); teachers in Common Core states may find W.6.1, W.7.1, and W.8.1 relevant (https://www.thecorestandards.org/ELA-Literacy/W/7/). Verify which version applies before adding codes.
- Lesson type: Interactive (browser), five parts meant to be done in order:
  1. **Spot the parts:** three short arguments about a made-up school (a later start time, phones at lunch, a school garden). Students choose a label and click each sentence it fits, then check. Every sentence gets an explanation; wrong labels get a guiding question instead of the answer. The parts appear in a different order each time (one argument opens with the counterclaim, one restates the claim at the end).
  2. **Sharpen the claim:** sort 12 statements into arguable claim, just a fact, personal taste, or too vague, with feedback on every choice. Then pick the strongest rewrite of three weak claims (“Phones are bad,” “Lunch stinks,” “Sports are good”).
  3. **Back it up:** for three claims, choose the strong evidence cards from five or six, then pick the reasoning sentence that connects them. Weak cards are named by type (bandwagon, one story, biased source, celebrity, off topic, opinion). Weak reasoning options repeat the evidence, are circular, lean on weak evidence, or jump to a new idea.
  4. **Answer the other side:** for three claims, pick the fairest counterclaim (against a straw man and an irrelevant point), then the best rebuttal (against “that’s just wrong,” an attack on the person, and giving up). Ends with a list of tricks that sound convincing but aren’t.
  5. **Build your own:** choose one of three questions (or bring your own topic), take a side, choose two evidence cards (weak cards and cards for the other side are explained, not added), write reasoning, a counterclaim, and a rebuttal with sentence starters. The paragraph assembles live with color-coded labels, quick checks, and a copy button.
- Target duration: About 45 minutes for all five. Parts 1 and 2 make a 15 to 20 minute opener; part 5 alone works as a 15 to 20 minute writing warm-up after the class has done parts 1 to 4.
- Setting: Whole-class on a projector (parts 1 to 4 work well as class votes), and individual or partner work on Chromebooks or tablets.
- Prior knowledge: Reading and writing a multi-sentence paragraph; the difference between a fact and an opinion.
- Key misconceptions:
  - Any opinion is a claim, or a fact can be the claim.
  - A stronger claim is a louder one (“really, really bad”).
  - Anything that sounds convincing is evidence (“everyone knows,” a celebrity, one story).
  - Evidence speaks for itself, so reasoning just repeats it.
  - Mentioning the other side weakens an argument.
  - A rebuttal means saying the other side is wrong, or criticizing the people.
  - Admitting part of a counterclaim means losing.
  - The claim must be the first sentence.
- Student actions and feedback: Label sentences and check; sort statements; choose evidence and reasoning; choose counterclaims and rebuttals; write a full paragraph. Every choice gets a specific explanation of why it is strong or weak, and wrong answers get a question that points to the fix.
- Formative assessment / evidence of learning: First-try counts and review lists at the end of parts 1 to 4; the paragraph from part 5, which students can copy into a document the teacher collects through the school’s usual system. Nothing is stored by the lesson.
- Accessibility / device constraints: All controls are buttons, checkboxes, or labeled text boxes and work by keyboard. Part labels always show their names, so color is never the only cue. Feedback uses polite live regions. Layouts stack at phone width. No animation. No external assets or network calls.
- Reliable educational sources: Common Core ELA Appendix A (argument versus persuasion); Toulmin, *The Uses of Argument*; McNeill and Krajcik (2012) on claim, evidence, and reasoning; Purdue OWL and UNC Writing Center argument guides; AAP (2014) for the one real fact. All passages and practice items are original. No textbook passages or question banks were copied.
- Known simplifications and limitations: See `lesson.json` `modelLimitations`. Most importantly, Ridgeview Middle School and its numbers are made up, which the lesson says on screen.
