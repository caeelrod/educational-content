# Teacher guide: Persuasive Writing

**Status:** Demo — not approved for classroom use yet.

## Outcomes
Students identify the five parts of an argument paragraph (claim, evidence, reasoning, counterclaim, rebuttal), tell an arguable claim from a fact, a taste, or a vague statement, choose strong evidence and explain why other evidence is weak, choose reasoning that explains the evidence, answer the other side fairly, and write a paragraph with all five parts.

The idea the lesson is built around: **persuasion is giving the reader good reasons, not saying it louder.** Each part has one job. Evidence is something a reader could check. Reasoning is the writer explaining what that evidence means for the claim. And the reader most worth persuading is the one who disagrees, which is why a fair counterclaim and a real rebuttal make an argument stronger.

The lesson says “persuasive writing” because that is the familiar term, and notes that many classes call it argument writing. The Common Core’s Appendix A draws a line between the two (argument leans on logic and evidence; persuasion can also lean on the writer’s credibility and the reader’s emotions). This lesson teaches the evidence-based kind.

## Before class
- Work through all five parts once (about 30 minutes for an adult).
- Ridgeview Middle School is made up. Its surveys, people, and numbers are invented, and the note under the lesson says so. Tell students not to quote Ridgeview numbers as real facts. The one real fact is the American Academy of Pediatrics’ 2014 recommendation that middle and high schools start at 8:30 a.m. or later.
- Decide whether students will copy their part 5 paragraph somewhere you can see it (a shared document or the school’s learning system). The lesson itself saves nothing and collects nothing.
- On a projector, part 1 works best at full width. On Chromebooks and phones, sentence labels move above each sentence.

## Facilitation steps
**Opener (3 minutes)**
1. Ask: “Think of a time you changed someone’s mind, or someone changed yours. What actually worked?” Collect answers; most will be reasons and facts, not volume.
2. Read the five parts in the legend at the top of the lesson aloud.

**1. Spot the parts (10 minutes)**
1. Do the first argument together on the projector. Have students call out a label for each sentence before you click.
2. Pause on sentences 3 and 4. Ask: “Which one could you check? Which one is the writer explaining?” That is the evidence and reasoning distinction.
3. Students do arguments 2 and 3 on their own or in pairs. Point out that argument 3 opens with the counterclaim and ends by restating the claim.

**2. Sharpen the claim (7 minutes)**
1. Sort the first few statements as a class vote (fingers 1 to 4 for the four kinds).
2. Discuss “Video games are bad”: many students will call it a claim. Ask “Bad how? For whom? What should happen?”
3. Students do “Fix weak claims” on their own.

**3. Back it up (10 minutes)**
1. Do the lunch claim together. Before checking, ask students to defend one card they chose and one they left out.
2. Name the tricks as they come up: bandwagon, one story, biased source. The “Tricks that sound convincing” box at the end lists them all.
3. For the reasoning step, ask why “The average student waits 11 minutes in line” isn’t reasoning. (It’s the evidence again.)

**4. Answer the other side (7 minutes)**
1. Ask first: “Does mentioning the other side make your argument weaker?” Take a vote, then revisit it at the end.
2. Point out the pattern in the best rebuttals: admit what’s fair, then answer with a reason or fact (“That’s a fair worry, but…”).

**5. Build your own (10 to 20 minutes)**
1. Students choose a question and a side. Encourage them to argue the side they *don’t* agree with at least once.
2. When they pick a card for the other side, the lesson tells them to save it for their counterclaim. Encourage that.
3. Students copy their paragraph into your collection document. Partners read each other’s paragraphs aloud and name each part.

## Discussion prompts
- What’s the difference between evidence and reasoning? Can you have one without the other? What goes wrong?
- Why is “Everyone knows lunch is too short” weaker than a count of how long students wait in line, even if both point the same way?
- When is one person’s story useful in an argument, and when is it not enough?
- Why would a writer start a paragraph with the other side’s view?
- Is it ever okay to admit the other side has a point? What do you gain?
- Think of an ad you’ve seen. Which parts of an argument does it have? Which tricks does it use?

## Misconceptions and how to address them
- **Any opinion is a claim / a fact can be a claim:** Part 2 sorts claims from tastes and facts, with an explanation for each, and notes that a fact can still be useful as evidence.
- **Louder means stronger:** “Phones are really, really bad” is an option in Fix weak claims, and its feedback says it is louder, not clearer.
- **Anything convincing is evidence:** Part 3 includes bandwagon, celebrity, biased-source, single-story, opinion, and off-topic cards, each named and explained.
- **Evidence speaks for itself:** Every claim in part 3 has a reasoning step, and the restate-the-evidence option is always wrong with an explanation. Part 5 warns if the reasoning copies the evidence.
- **Mentioning the other side is weak:** Part 4 opens by addressing this, and its summary explains who the writer is really trying to persuade.
- **A rebuttal is saying they’re wrong, or criticizing them:** Part 4’s rebuttal options always include “that’s just wrong” and an attack on the person. Part 5 flags insults and rebuttals with no reason.
- **Admitting a point is giving up:** The best rebuttals in part 4 start by admitting the concern is real; the “give up” option shows the difference.
- **The claim goes first:** Part 1’s third argument opens with the counterclaim.

## Assessment / expected responses
- Part 1, argument 1: claim, evidence, evidence, reasoning, counterclaim, rebuttal. Argument 2: claim, evidence, counterclaim, rebuttal, evidence, reasoning. Argument 3: counterclaim, rebuttal, claim, evidence, reasoning, claim (restated).
- Strong explanation of evidence versus reasoning: “Evidence is the fact or number. Reasoning is where I explain why that fact proves my point.”
- A good part 5 paragraph (start time, yes): “Ridgeview should start school at 8:30 a.m. instead of 7:45. In 2014, the American Academy of Pediatrics recommended that middle and high schools start at 8:30 a.m. or later. In a survey of 300 Ridgeview students, 6 out of 10 said they feel sleepy during first period. This shows that the early start is keeping students from being ready to learn, and doctors agree a later start would help. Some families worry that a later start would push practices and jobs later into the evening. That’s a fair point, but practices would only move 45 minutes, and students who are awake in class will learn more.”
- Watch for: reasoning that just restates the claim; a counterclaim that is a straw man; a rebuttal that only says “that’s wrong.”

## Accommodations and extension ideas
- Support: Do parts 1 to 4 as a whole class, then let students write part 5 with a partner. Sentence starters are built in for reasoning and rebuttal, and a suggested counterclaim is available for the three given questions.
- Support: Everything works by keyboard; students who use speech-to-text can dictate into the part 5 boxes.
- Challenge: In part 5, argue both sides of the same question, then decide which paragraph is more convincing and why.
- Challenge: Use “My own topic” and find two pieces of real evidence from a source the teacher approves. Note where each came from.
- Extension: Expand the paragraph into a short essay with an introduction (hook and claim), two body paragraphs with different reasons, a counterclaim paragraph, and a conclusion.
- Extension: Find an ad, editorial, or speech and label its parts. Which tricks from the list does it use?

## Simplifications / limitations
- Ridgeview Middle School and all its people, surveys, and numbers are invented for practice.
- The lesson works on one body paragraph with five parts. Full essays have more: an introduction and hook, several reasons, transitions, and a conclusion.
- In part 1 each sentence has exactly one label. In real writing a sentence can do two jobs, and reasoning can span several sentences.
- Part 3 sorts evidence into strong and weak for one claim. Real evidence strength is a matter of degree.
- Part 5’s quick checks only look for surface features (length, linking words, a short list of insults). They don’t judge whether the argument is convincing, and the lesson says so.
- Terms vary by curriculum: “reasons,” “warrant,” “opposing claim,” “refutation.” Tell students which words your class uses.
