# Teacher guide: Plate Tectonics

**Status:** Demo — not approved for classroom use yet.

## Outcomes
Students explain, using evidence, that Earth's outer shell is broken into plates that move a few centimeters a year. They calculate a spreading rate from a distance and an age. They identify divergent, convergent (ocean-continent, ocean-ocean, continent-continent), and transform boundaries from their landforms, earthquakes, and volcanoes, and explain why each boundary produces what it does.

The story the lesson follows is the one scientists followed: Wegener's evidence that continents moved (part 1), the missing mechanism found on the ocean floor (part 2), then what happens at the edges of plates (parts 3 and 4).

## Before class
- Work through all four parts once. In part 1, try both dragging Africa and using the keyboard (click Africa or Tab to it, then arrow keys and Q/E). **Show me the fit** completes the puzzle if a student is stuck.
- In part 2, try **You do** (flip the field a few times while it runs) and **Earth's real record**. Then try **Fast ridge** to see that the same time makes wider bands.
- In part 3, run each boundary once. On a projector, turn off **Earthquakes** first to keep the picture simple, then turn it on to discuss depth patterns.
- On phones the cross-sections scroll sideways. On Chromebooks and projectors they fit the screen.
- Nothing is saved, and no student information is collected. Scores disappear when the page closes.

## Facilitation steps
**Part 1: Continent puzzle (10 minutes)**
1. Before showing anything, ask: "Have you ever noticed that some continents look like they could fit together?" Then let students fit Africa against South America.
2. Turn on one clue at a time. For Mesosaurus, ask: "This animal lived in shallow water. How could its fossils end up on both sides of an ocean?" Take ideas (land bridges, swimming, rafting) before discussing.
3. Read Wegener's argument and the objection. Ask: "If you were a scientist in 1920, what would you need to see before you believed this?"

**Part 2: Seafloor spreading (10 to 15 minutes)**
1. Open **How does rock record magnetism?** and read it together.
2. Run **You do** and have students call out when to flip the field. Stop and ask the noticing question.
3. Switch to **Earth's real record** and point out the pattern is not regular: reversals happen at uneven times.
4. Do **Measure how fast plates move** individually or in pairs. Afterward, compare with fingernail growth.

**Part 3: Boundary lab (10 to 15 minutes)**
1. Read **What is a plate?** together, stopping on "Plates do not float on a sea of lava."
2. For each boundary, have students commit to a prediction (hands up or a quick poll) before pressing **Run time**.
3. On ocean-continent, ask students to describe the earthquake pattern. Lead them to: shallow near the trench, deeper inland, following the sinking plate.
4. On continent-continent, ask why the volcanoes stop after the collision.
5. On the transform fault, pause just before an earthquake (the road is bent) and ask what will happen next.

**Part 4: Boundary detective (10 minutes)**
1. Students do one round of 6 individually or in pairs. Encourage them to name the clue that decided it.
2. Share out the hardest case. Hawaii and the Appalachians usually spark the best discussion.

## Discussion prompts
- Why was "the coastlines fit" not enough evidence on its own?
- New ocean floor is made at ridges all the time, but Earth isn't getting bigger. Where does old ocean floor go?
- Why is the oldest ocean floor only about 200 million years old when some continental rocks are about 4 billion years old?
- Why does an ocean plate sink under a continent, but two continents don't sink under each other?
- If plates move steadily, why do earthquakes happen suddenly?
- Hawaii's volcanoes are in the middle of a plate. How does the island chain show which way the Pacific Plate is moving?
- Where do you think the next ocean might form?

## Misconceptions and how to address them
- **Plates float on lava:** The "What is a plate?" panel says directly that the mantle is almost entirely solid, that the asthenosphere flows slowly even though it is solid, and that magma forms only in certain places. The cross-sections show magma only under ridges, in rifts, and above sinking plates.
- **Continents plow through the ocean floor:** Part 1 ends by saying Wegener's guess was wrong; part 2's takeaway says the ocean floor itself moves and carries continents along.
- **Plates move only during earthquakes, or fast:** Part 2's speed check gives centimeters per year; the transform model shows steady motion far from the fault and sudden slips at it.
- **Boundaries are coastlines:** The puzzle shows the Atlantic coasts fit together with no boundary between them today; the spreading part shows the boundary is the ridge in the middle of the ocean. The Appalachians case names the Mid-Atlantic Ridge as the nearest boundary.
- **Earth is getting bigger:** The takeaway at the end of part 2 sets up subduction, which part 3 shows.
- **All volcanoes are at boundaries:** The Hawaii case.
- **Ocean floor is as old as continents:** Part 2's second noticing question gives about 200 million years versus about 4 billion years.
- **Every convergent boundary makes volcanoes:** Continent-continent collision and the Himalaya case.
- **Mountains mark today's boundaries:** The Appalachians case.

## Assessment / expected responses
- Evidence for plate motion: continent shapes fit; the same fossils (Mesosaurus, Glossopteris) and the same old rocks appear on both sides; glacier marks in now-warm places line up; magnetic stripes are mirror images on both sides of ridges; ocean floor is youngest at ridges and gets older away from them.
- Speed check: speed (cm per year) = distance (km) ÷ age (million years) ÷ 10. For example, 20 km at 0.8 million years is 25 km per million years = 25 mm per year = 2.5 cm per year for one side, 5 cm per year for the whole ocean.
- Boundary reasoning: a trench means a plate is sinking; volcanoes in mountains on land mean ocean under continent; a curved chain of volcanic islands means ocean under ocean; huge mountains with few volcanoes mean continent-continent; offset streams and fences with shallow earthquakes and no volcanoes mean transform; young rock in the middle and shallow earthquakes mean divergent.
- Strong explanation of subduction: "Ocean plate is denser than continent, so it sinks. As it goes down, it releases water that helps the mantle above it melt, which feeds the volcanoes."

## Accommodations and extension ideas
- Support: Do parts 1 and 3 only, as a whole class. Leave **Labels** on. Use **Show me the fit**. In part 4, read the clues aloud.
- Support: Keyboard users can do every activity with Tab, arrow keys, Enter, and Q/E in the puzzle.
- Challenge: In part 2, ask how long it would take to open an ocean 3,000 km wide at 3 cm per year (100 million years), and compare with when the South Atlantic began to open.
- Challenge: Ask students to write a clue card for a place not in the detective cases (for example, Japan, the Alps, or the Dead Sea) and test it on a partner.
- Extension: Look up a recent earthquake and decide what kind of boundary caused it from its location and depth.

## Simplifications / limitations
- The continent fit is done on a flat map with simplified coastlines, so it is close but not exact. Scientists fit the edges of the continental shelves on a globe.
- Clue areas are approximate.
- The spreading view uses straight bands and instant reversals. Real bands are wavy and offset, and reversals take thousands of years.
- Cross-sections are not to scale, depths are stretched, and boundaries between layers are drawn as sharp lines.
- Plates move evenly in the cross-sections. The transform model shows stick-slip behavior, with equal earthquakes at regular intervals.
- What drives plates is simplified to slab pull, ridge push, and mantle flow in one sentence; scientists still study how much each contributes.
- Detective cases simplify places that involve more than one process (for example, Iceland is on a ridge and over a hot spot, and the Aleutians change from ocean-ocean to ocean-continent from west to east).
