# Teacher guide: Ratios and Proportions

**Status:** Demo — not approved for classroom use yet.

## Outcomes
Students explain why equivalent ratios describe the same mix, find missing values with ratio tables, double number lines, scale factors, and unit rates, compare rates by finding unit rates, tell part-to-part ratios from part-to-whole fractions, and decide whether a relationship is proportional from a table or a graph, giving k in y = kx.

The idea the lesson is built around: **a bigger batch is copies of the same recipe.** Multiplying both parts by the same number makes copies, so the mix stays the same. Adding the same amount to both parts doesn't make copies, so the mix changes. Every later part (tables, unit rates, y = kx) is this idea written a different way: a unit rate is the recipe scaled to one, and k is that unit rate.

## Before class
- Do the eight paint missions once. Mission 3 (Sam's shortcut) is the one to slow down on.
- In **Tables and number lines**, press **+1 to both** once so you know what the leaning red line looks like.
- In **Unit rates**, run a few races. Most are set up so the runner who went farther is slower.
- In **Proportional or not?**, set the booking fee above $0 and watch the y ÷ x column change.
- On a projector, the buckets, number lines, and graphs read best at full width. On phones everything stacks.
- Nothing is saved, and no student information is collected. Scores disappear when the page closes.

### About the paint colors
The app colors paint using one number only: what fraction of the bucket is blue. That makes the key idea visible (same ratio, same share, same color), but real paint is more complicated: pigments differ in strength, and some don't mix to a clean green. If students ask, say it's a model: it shows what stays the same when a ratio stays the same, and a paint store follows the same rule when it scales a recipe.

## Facilitation steps
**Opener: Mix it (10 to 15 minutes)**
1. Project mission 1 and let a student mix it. Ask the class: "Will 2 blue and 4 yellow make the same color? What about 5 and 10?"
2. Mission 2: before anyone checks, ask for predictions. Someone usually says 4 blue and 5 yellow. Let them check it; the feedback names the "added 3 to each" move.
3. Mission 3: take a class vote on Sam's shortcut before revealing. Then show "What Sam should mix" and point at the cans: two copies of the recipe.
4. Missions 5 to 7 work well as quick votes. For mission 5, ask how to compare without the share bar (make the blue the same: 15 : 20 vs 15 : 24).
5. Mission 8 sets up the "groups" strategy used in part-to-whole problems later.

**Tables and number lines (10 minutes)**
1. Start with Lemonade. Make ×2 and ×3 rows, then **Make it 1**. Ask what 2 2/3 means (cups of water for 1 cup of lemon juice).
2. Press **+1 to both** and ask why the red line leans.
3. Students answer the three questions on their own. The third question in each situation needs a unit rate or a decimal scale factor.

**Unit rates (10 minutes)**
1. Run a race on the projector and take a vote. After the reveal, ask: "Why didn't the person who ran farther win?"
2. Point out the two unit rates in the table. Ask which one means "faster" when it's bigger and which when it's smaller.
3. Do one or two better-buy pairs the same way.

**Proportional or not? (10 minutes, often a later day)**
1. In the explorer, set a booking fee and ask what changed in the table and the graph. Set it back to $0.
2. Students sort the eight situations. The taxi and the square are the ones to discuss: the taxi is a straight line that misses (0, 0); the square goes through (0, 0) but curves.

**Practice (10 minutes)**
1. Students do a round of 6 on their own. Encourage **Set it up as a ratio table** at first.
2. Afterward, ask which kind of problem they slipped on. It's usually part-to-whole or a flipped unit rate.

## Discussion prompts
- What has to stay the same when you make a bigger batch of a recipe? What can change?
- Sam says "I added the same amount of each color, so it's fair." What would you tell Sam?
- Is 2 : 3 the same as 2/3? When can you use a ratio as a fraction, and what does the fraction mean?
- Ana ran farther than Ben. Does that mean Ana is faster? What else do you need to know?
- Why are there two unit rates for every rate? When would you use each one?
- Name a real situation that is proportional and one that looks proportional but isn't.

## Misconceptions and how to address them
- **Additive reasoning (2 : 3 → 4 : 5):** Mission 2 and mission 3 in the paint shop, the "+1 to both" trap on the number lines, and practice feedback that detects an "added the difference" answer.
- **Subtracting to simplify (6 : 4 → 4 : 2):** Mission 4 detects 4 : 2 and explains it.
- **Order doesn't matter:** Mission 6 shows 2 : 5 and 5 : 2 side by side.
- **Part-to-part read as part-to-whole:** Mission 7 and the part-to-whole practice problems, which detect "used the total as one part" and "found the other part".
- **More of something means a bigger share:** Mission 5 compares 3 : 4 with 5 : 8 and shows the "make one part the same" strategy.
- **Farther is faster / bigger box is cheaper:** The race and shop generators make most matchups traps, and feedback says why the obvious pick fails.
- **Rates divided the wrong way:** Unit-rate practice detects the flipped rate; races and shopping show both unit rates and what "bigger is better" means for each.
- **Straight line or through-origin means proportional:** The explorer's fee slider; the taxi (line, not through origin) and square (through origin, curved) situations in the sort.
- **k as x ÷ y:** The k question in the sort and the constant-of-proportionality practice problem detect 1/k.

## Assessment / expected responses
- Strong explanation of equivalent ratios: "4 : 6 is two copies of 2 : 3, so each copy has the same amount of blue for its yellow. 4 : 5 isn't copies of anything; it has more blue for each yellow."
- Lemonade (3 cups lemon juice : 8 cups water): 12 cups lemon juice → 32 cups water; 40 cups water → 15 cups lemon juice; 4.5 cups lemon juice → 12 cups water.
- Map (2 cm : 15 km): 5 cm → 37.5 km.
- 2 blue : 3 yellow, 15 cans total: 6 blue, 9 yellow. Blue is 2/5 of the paint.
- 300 m in 60 s vs 360 m in 90 s: 5 m/s vs 4 m/s, so the first runner is faster.
- Taxi with $3 start and $2 per mile: not proportional (5 ÷ 1 = 5 but 7 ÷ 2 = 3.5; costs $3 at 0 miles).
- Jordan earns $11 per hour: proportional, k = 11, y = 11x.

## Accommodations and extension ideas
- Support: Stay in the paint shop until students can say "multiply both" in their own words. Use the tape diagram of cans to show copies.
- Support: In parts 2 and 5, use the ratio table every time. Typed answers accept fractions, decimals, mixed numbers, and dollar amounts.
- Support: Everything works by keyboard, and nothing is timed.
- Challenge: In the paint shop, is 3 : 4 or 5 : 7 bluer? Find two ways to decide.
- Challenge: Write an equation for the taxi fare. Why isn't it of the form y = kx?
- Extension: Cross products. If 3/8 = 12/x, show why 3 × x = 8 × 12 gives the same answer as the scale factor (OpenStax *Prealgebra 2e*, Section 6.5).
- Extension: Percent as a ratio to 100. What percent of the 2 : 3 paint is blue?

## Simplifications / limitations
- Paint color depends only on the share of blue (see "About the paint colors" above).
- Runners move at constant speed for the whole race.
- Ratio tables only offer ×2, ×3, ×10, ÷2, and "Make it 1".
- Practice uses 8 templates with random numbers. Percent problems, scale drawings with area, and inverse proportion are not included, and cross-multiplication is left as an extension rather than taught as a procedure.
- A decimal within 0.006 of a non-terminating answer (such as 13.33 for 13 1/3) is accepted.
