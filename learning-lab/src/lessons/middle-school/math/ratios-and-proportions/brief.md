# Lesson brief: Ratios and Proportions

- Section / subject / level: Middle School / Math / Grades 6–7 (ratio and rate reasoning, then proportional relationships). Confirm placement with the teacher.
- Student learning objective (observable): The student explains why equivalent ratios make the same mix ("both parts were multiplied by the same number"); finds a missing value with a ratio table, double number line, scale factor, or unit rate; compares two rates by finding unit rates; tells a part-to-part ratio from a part-to-whole fraction; and decides whether a relationship is proportional from a table (is y ÷ x always the same?) or a graph (a straight line through (0, 0)?), stating k in y = kx when it is.
- Topic / motivating question: If you make a bigger batch of a recipe, what has to stay the same, and how do you keep it the same?
- Standards (jurisdiction + version/year + exact code + URL; or TBD): TBD. Ratios, rates, and proportional relationships are common grade 6 and grade 7 topics, but no code has been checked. Verify against the Oklahoma Academic Standards for Mathematics at https://oklahoma.gov/education/services/standards-learning/oklahoma-academic-standards.html with the teacher before attaching anything.
- Lesson type: Interactive (browser), five parts meant to be done in order:
  1. **Mix it (paint shop):** eight short missions. Match a swatch from a recipe, make a bigger batch with a fixed amount of blue, predict whether Sam's "add 2 of each" shortcut keeps the color, find the fewest cans, compare 3 : 4 with 5 : 8, check whether order matters, find the part-to-whole fraction, and fill an order of 15 cans. The paint color depends only on the share of blue, so equivalent ratios look identical and additive mistakes look visibly off. A tape diagram of cans groups the mix into copies of the simplest recipe, and a blue-share bar marks the target.
  2. **Tables and number lines:** four situations (lemonade, trail mix, map scale, pancakes). Students build a ratio table with ×2, ×3, ×10, ÷2, and "Make it 1", and every row appears on a double number line where equivalent pairs line up. A "+1 to both" button adds a crossed-out row whose marks visibly lean. Three missing-value questions per situation, with feedback for additive and upside-down answers, then both a scale-factor and a unit-rate explanation.
  3. **Unit rates:** "Who is faster?" (predict, then watch both runners for 10 seconds at their own speeds, then see meters per second and seconds per meter) and "Which is the better buy?" (predict, then see dollars per ounce and ounces per dollar). Most matchups are built so the farther runner, or the cheaper total, is the wrong pick.
  4. **Proportional or not?:** an explorer where ticket price and a booking fee are sliders; the y ÷ x column and the graph show that any fee breaks proportionality. Then eight situations to sort from the table alone (four proportional; a taxi fare with a starting fee, square area, sibling ages, and a burning candle are not). The graph and y ÷ x column appear after each answer, and proportional ones ask for k.
  5. **Practice:** rounds of 6 randomly generated word problems from 8 templates (scale up, unit price, unit rate, part to whole, map scale, constant of proportionality from a table, scale down, better buy). Optional "Set it up as a ratio table" scaffold, mistake-specific feedback, "Show me how" after two misses, and "Redo the ones I slipped on".
- Target duration: About 45 minutes for all five. Part 1 alone is a 10 to 15 minute opener; parts 2 and 3 fit a ratio-and-rate day; part 4 fits a later day on proportional relationships (grade 7); part 5 is 10 minutes of practice.
- Setting: Projector or interactive whiteboard for whole-class prediction (parts 1, 3, and 4 work well with a class vote), or individual Chromebooks and tablets.
- Prior knowledge: Multiplying and dividing whole numbers and decimals, simple fractions, reading a table, and plotting points.
- Key misconceptions:
  - Adding the same amount to both parts keeps the ratio (2 : 3 → 4 : 5), or subtracting the same amount simplifies it (6 : 4 → 4 : 2).
  - Order doesn't matter in a ratio.
  - In 2 : 3, blue is 2/3 of the paint (part-to-part read as part-to-whole).
  - More of something means a bigger share of it (5 : 8 looks bluer than 3 : 4).
  - Farther means faster; a bigger box or a lower total price means a better buy.
  - A rate can be divided either way without changing what it means.
  - Any increasing relationship or any straight-line graph is proportional; any graph through the origin is proportional.
  - k is x ÷ y, or the jump between columns.
- Student actions and feedback: Mix paint with +/− buttons and check against a target; predict before every reveal; build table rows and watch them line up on the number lines; try the "+1 to both" trap; type missing values with feedback naming the specific slip; race and shop with unit rates shown both ways; move sliders to make a relationship proportional or not; sort situations and find k; solve randomly generated practice problems.
- Formative assessment / evidence of learning: Mission predictions in part 1, missing-value answers in part 2, race and shop predictions in part 3, sort score in part 4, and the practice round summary (first-try count, each problem with its answer, and a redo option). Nothing is stored after the page closes.
- Accessibility / device constraints: All controls are labeled buttons, sliders, or text inputs and work by keyboard; answer inputs submit with Enter and focus moves to the next control. Feedback uses polite live regions. Buckets, number lines, graphs, and the race have text labels describing what they show. The race and color transitions are turned off under reduced motion. Layouts stack at phone width, and number-line labels get larger.
- Reliable educational sources: OpenStax *Prealgebra 2e*, Section 5.6 (Ratios and Rate) and Section 6.5 (Solve Proportions and their Applications); Illustrative Mathematics, IM 6–8 Math Grade 7 Unit 2 teacher guide, for the definitions of proportional relationship and constant of proportionality. The paint-mixing context is a classic classroom task for ratio reasoning; the missions, situations, and practice templates here are original. No textbook images or problem banks were copied.
- Known simplifications and limitations: See `lesson.json` `modelLimitations`. The biggest one: the app colors paint by the share of blue only, which real paint doesn't do exactly. The teacher guide says how to frame it.
