# Teacher guide: Supply and Demand

**Status:** Demo — not approved for classroom use yet.

## Outcomes
Students explain where demand and supply curves come from (individual buyers' and sellers' limits), find the equilibrium price and quantity and explain why a market moves toward it, classify a news event as a shift in demand, a shift in supply, or a movement along a curve, predict the new equilibrium, and predict the effect of a price ceiling or floor.

The explanation the lesson is built around: **at any price other than equilibrium there is a shortage or a surplus, and that gap pushes the price.** A shortage pushes it up; a surplus pushes it down. When a curve shifts, look at the old price: the new shortage or surplus there tells you which way the price goes, and the curve that did not move tells you what happens to quantity.

## Before class
- Work through all five parts once. Parts 1 to 4 are guided exploration; part 5 is practice.
- In **Buyers and growers**, try $4, $5, and $5.50 in "Both together" and notice that only $5 makes the counts match.
- In **Shift or slide?**, events 5 and 8 are traps: they describe a change in the strawberry price itself. Decide in advance how you want students to say it ("a change in quantity demanded", "a movement along the curve").
- On a projector, use full width. On phones the charts redraw with larger labels and the layout stacks.
- Nothing is saved and no student information is collected. Practice scores disappear when the page closes.

## Facilitation steps
**Opener (3 minutes)**
1. Ask: "After a frost, strawberries cost more at every stand. Who decided that?" Collect answers (the farmers, the stores, the government). Return to this at the end of part 3.

**Buyers and growers (10 minutes)**
1. In "Buyers", raise the price slowly. Ask: "Who drops out first, and why them?" Answer the check question together.
2. In "Growers", ask why the heated greenhouse only sells at a high price. Introduce cost as "the least a seller would accept", including what else they could do with the time or land (the blueberry grower).
3. In "Both together", have students find the matching price. Point to the takeaway: thousands of buyers turn the staircase into a curve.

**Find the price (10 minutes)**
1. Start at $2. Have students predict the direction, then press "Let the market adjust". Repeat from $7.50.
2. Ask: "What is a buyer doing at $2 that pushes the price up? What is a grower doing at $7.50 that pushes it down?"
3. Read "What equilibrium does not mean" aloud. Emphasize that equilibrium is a description, not a judgment.

**Shift or slide? (15 minutes)**
1. Model event 1 (frost) with the class, saying each of the four steps out loud.
2. Students do events 2 to 8 in pairs. Stop after event 5 to discuss the trap.
3. Use "Shift both curves yourself" to show the tug of war: with both curves shifting right, quantity must rise but price depends on sizes.
4. Return to the opener: nobody decided the frost price. Supply shifted left, a shortage appeared at the old price, and the price rose until it cleared.

**Ceilings and floors (10 minutes)**
1. Have students predict all four scenarios on paper before revealing any.
2. Discuss the surprise in the binding ceiling: fewer boxes change hands than without it.
3. For AP or advanced students, turn on "Go further" and compare the three areas with and without the rule.

**Read the news (10 to 15 minutes, or homework)**
1. Students play at least one round on their own. Afterward ask which kind of headline they slipped on most.

## Discussion prompts
- Why does it matter that the bakery appears twice on the buyer list?
- What is the difference between "demand fell" and "quantity demanded fell"? Give an example of each.
- A heat wave hits. Does it shift the demand or the supply of ice cream? Why is "it affects ice cream, so supply" the wrong reasoning?
- If both curves shift right, why can't you know what happens to price without more information?
- A price ceiling is meant to help buyers. Which buyers does it help, and which does it hurt?
- Price ceilings and floors in the real world (rent control, the minimum wage, farm price supports) are debated. What would you need to know about a real market to judge one? (Point toward evidence: how responsive buyers and sellers are, what else changes, and who is affected.)

## Misconceptions and how to address them
- **A price change shifts the curve:** Events 5 and 8 in part 3 and one trap per practice round; feedback says price is on the axis, so a price change is a slide along the curve. The teacher-facing vocabulary note appears after each trap.
- **Whoever "feels" the news is the curve that shifts:** Part 3 and practice feedback ask "whose plans change first?" and list the demand shifters (tastes, incomes, related goods' prices, number of buyers, expectations) and supply shifters (input costs, technology, weather, number of sellers, taxes and subsidies, sellers' other options).
- **Supply decrease means "down":** The direction feedback for supply-left events notes that a supply curve shifting left looks higher, so the lesson uses left and right.
- **Price and quantity always move together, or supply up means price up:** The prediction step in part 3 explains each direction through the shortage or surplus at the old price and the curve that did not move.
- **Two shifts give two certain answers:** "Shift both curves yourself" and the double-shift practice item; practice feedback explains the tug of war.
- **Equilibrium means everyone gets the good, or is fair:** "What equilibrium does not mean" in part 2.
- **Any ceiling causes a shortage:** The $5 ceiling and $3 floor scenarios are non-binding, with feedback explaining why.
- **A ceiling lets more people buy:** Part 4 readouts show fewer boxes actually sold under any binding ceiling.

## Assessment / expected responses
- Strong explanation of equilibrium: "At a price below $4 buyers want more than growers bring, so some buyers offer more and sellers raise prices. Above $4 there are leftovers and sellers cut prices. Only at $4 does neither happen."
- Late frost: supply shifts left; at the old price there is a shortage; price rises and quantity falls ($4 → $5, 10,000 → 8,000 boxes in the model).
- Blueberries on sale: demand for strawberries shifts left (substitute); price and quantity both fall.
- "Strawberry prices rise and shoppers buy fewer": no shift; a decrease in quantity demanded along the demand curve.
- Drought plus a popular coffee study: supply left and demand right; price rises; quantity can't be determined without the sizes.
- Ceiling at $3: shortage of 5,000 boxes; only 7,000 boxes sold. Ceiling at $5: no effect. Floor at $5: surplus of 5,000 boxes.

## Accommodations and extension ideas
- Support: Stay in part 1 longer; have students point to the one buyer who drops out at each step. In part 3, give a printed list of demand and supply shifters.
- Support: Everything works by keyboard; tapping the chart is optional.
- Challenge: With the model's numbers, compute the new equilibrium algebraically after demand shifts right by 5,000 (18,000 + 5,000 − 2,000P = 3,000P − 2,000 gives P = $5).
- Challenge: In "Go further", explain why deadweight loss grows as the ceiling is set lower.
- Extension: Introduce price elasticity: what changes in part 3 if the supply curve were steeper?
- Extension: OpenStax 3.4 discusses rent control and farm price supports; have students read it and connect each to part 4.

## Simplifications / limitations
- The market is made up: straight-line curves (quantity demanded = 18,000 − 2,000 × price; quantity supplied = 3,000 × price − 2,000, in boxes per week), one kind of box, and a price everyone can see.
- Every shift in part 3 and the practice sketches is the same size (5,000 boxes) so the new prices are whole dollars. Real shifts vary in size.
- The price-adjust animation closes 30% of the gap each step. It shows the direction of pressure, not how fast or how real markets adjust.
- Part 1 counts a buyer whose top price equals the price as buying, and likewise for growers.
- The consumer surplus shading under a ceiling assumes the scarce boxes go to the buyers who value them most; the text notes this.
- The model draws no lines, black markets, or quality changes; the text describes them.
- Rent control and the minimum wage are mentioned as examples, with a note that real markets are more complicated and their effects are studied and debated. The lesson takes no position on either policy.
- Income effects assume a normal good for strawberries; the practice bank includes one inferior good, labeled in the headline.
