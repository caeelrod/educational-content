# Lesson brief: Supply and Demand

- Section / subject / level: High School / Economics / a semester Economics course (typically grades 11–12). Also a first-week review for AP Microeconomics or Macroeconomics; part 4's "Go further" box previews consumer surplus, producer surplus, and deadweight loss.
- Student learning objective (observable): Given a news event about a market, the student says whether it shifts demand, shifts supply, or is only a change in the good's own price (a movement along a curve); says which way the curve shifts; predicts the change in equilibrium price and quantity and explains it through the shortage or surplus at the old price; and predicts whether a price ceiling or floor causes a shortage, a surplus, or no effect.
- Topic / motivating question: Why do strawberries cost more after a frost, when nobody sets the price?
- Standards: TBD. Candidate references to verify with the teacher before attaching codes: Oklahoma Academic Standards for Social Studies, high school Economics (price and non-price factors in supply and demand; shortages and surpluses, including price ceilings and floors); and the Council for Economic Education *Voluntary National Content Standards in Economics* (2nd ed., 2010), Standards 7 (Markets and Prices) and 8 (Role of Prices). See `curriculum/high-school/scope-and-sequence.md`. `standards` is left empty in `lesson.json` and `registry.ts`.
- Lesson type: Interactive (browser), five parts meant to be done in order:
  1. **Buyers and growers:** twelve buyers (the most each would pay for a box) and twelve growers (the least each would accept). A price slider shows who buys and who sells, drawn as demand and supply staircases. Check questions on the law of demand and why supply slopes up; then find the one price where the counts match ($5, 7 boxes). A small figure shows staircases becoming smooth curves as the number of buyers grows.
  2. **Find the price:** the whole-town market with straight-line curves. Pick a price (slider or tap the chart), read quantity demanded, quantity supplied, the shortage or surplus, and boxes actually sold. Predict which way the price will move, then let the market adjust. Ends with "what equilibrium does not mean".
  3. **Shift or slide?:** twelve events in the strawberry town (weather, tastes, substitutes, complements, technology, input costs, income, expectations, number of sellers, a per-box fee, and two price-only traps). Students pick the curve, then the direction; the curve slides over and the shortage or surplus at the old price appears; they predict price and quantity, then see the new equilibrium. A second activity shifts both curves with sliders to show that one result is certain and the other depends on sizes.
  4. **Ceilings and floors:** four predict-first scenarios (binding and non-binding ceiling and floor), then free exploration. Shows the shortage or surplus, boxes actually sold, and the consequences the model does not draw (lines, under-the-table payments, quality cuts, government purchases of surplus). Optional "Go further" box shades consumer surplus, producer surplus, and deadweight loss with dollar values.
  5. **Read the news:** rounds of six headlines from other markets (gasoline, coffee, apartments, sneakers, and more): four single shifts, one price-only trap, and one double shift. Students mark each curve left / no shift / right, then price and quantity rises / falls / can't tell, with feedback aimed at the specific slip and a "Show me" option after two misses.
- Target duration: About 50 minutes for all five. Parts 1 and 2 work alone as a 20-minute introduction; part 3 is the core of a second day; part 5 alone is 10 to 15 minutes of review.
- Setting: Whole-class on a projector, individual work on Chromebooks or phones, and homework review.
- Prior knowledge: Reading a graph with two axes. No economics background.
- Key misconceptions:
  - A change in a good's own price shifts its demand or supply curve (confusing "demand" with "quantity demanded").
  - Any news shifts demand, or whoever "feels" the news is the curve that shifts (e.g. a heat wave shifts ice cream supply).
  - A supply decrease is a shift "down" (it looks higher on the chart).
  - Demand rising means price and quantity can move in opposite directions; or supply rising raises price.
  - When two curves shift, both price and quantity can always be predicted.
  - Equilibrium means everyone gets the good, or that the price is fair.
  - Any price ceiling causes a shortage, including one set above equilibrium.
  - A price ceiling lets more people buy the good.
- Student actions and feedback: Move the price and watch individual buyers and growers respond; answer check questions with explanations for each option; predict the direction of price adjustment; classify events step by step with feedback naming the shifting factor; predict ceiling and floor outcomes; classify headlines with targeted hints.
- Formative assessment / evidence of learning: Check questions in part 1; the prediction in part 2; the event sequence in part 3 (checkmarks on completed events); ceiling and floor predictions in part 4; practice round score and the list of headlines the student slipped on, with "Redo the ones I slipped on". Nothing is stored.
- Accessibility / device constraints: Every control is a button, slider, or checkbox with a label and works by keyboard; tapping the chart is an extra, never the only way. Charts have text labels for each curve (not color alone) and plain-language `aria-label`s that state the current numbers; readouts and feedback use polite live regions. The curve-shift animation is a short CSS transition, turned off under reduced motion; the price-adjust animation jumps to the end under reduced motion. Charts redraw at phone width with larger labels.
- Reliable educational sources: OpenStax *Principles of Economics 3e*, Chapter 3 (3.1 Demand, Supply, and Equilibrium in Markets for Goods and Services; 3.2 Shifts in Demand and Supply for Goods and Services; 3.3 Changes in Equilibrium Price and Quantity: The Four-Step Process; 3.4 Price Ceilings and Price Floors; 3.5 Demand, Supply, and Efficiency). Council for Economic Education, *Voluntary National Content Standards in Economics*, 2nd ed. (2010), Standards 7 and 8 and their grade 12 benchmarks. The strawberry market, buyers, growers, numbers, and headlines are original to this lesson; no textbook figures or problem sets were copied.
