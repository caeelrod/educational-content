# Teacher guide: Newton's Laws of Motion

**Status:** Demo — not approved for classroom use yet.

## Outcomes
Students use Newton's three laws to explain and predict motion:
- **First law:** explain that an object keeps a constant velocity (including staying at rest) when the net force on it is zero, and that everyday objects stop because of friction, not because motion runs out.
- **Second law:** analyze their own data to support a = F_net / m, and use F_net = ma to find acceleration, net force, or mass.
- **Third law:** identify the partner of a force by swapping the two objects, and explain why partners don't cancel (they act on different objects).
- **Free-body diagrams:** draw the forces on one object, naming the source of each, and choose the direction of the net force from how the velocity is changing.

The idea the lesson is built around: **forces change motion; they are not needed to keep it.** Most students arrive with the opposite intuition, and every part returns to it.

## Before class
- Work through all six parts once. Parts 1 to 5 are exploration; part 6 is practice.
- In part 1, try the wooden floor at a hold-to-push force of exactly 2.0 N: once the block is moving, it keeps a steady speed.
- In part 2, run 1 N, 2 N, and 3 N with a 1 kg cart. The measured accelerations include a small random error on purpose, so no two classes get identical tables.
- On a projector, set the browser to full width; on phones, parts stack and drawings redraw with larger labels.
- Nothing is saved, and no student information is collected.

## Facilitation steps
**Opener (3 minutes).** Ask: “If you kick a ball across an endless, perfectly smooth floor, what happens?” Take a vote before opening the lesson.

**1. First law: keep sliding (8 minutes)**
1. Shove the block on wood, then carpet, then ice. Ask what changed and what stayed the same (the shove was the same; only friction changed).
2. Have students make the frictionless prediction, then test it. Discuss why it never stops.
3. Challenge: steady speed on wood. Ask what the net force is while the speed is constant. (Zero.)

**2. Second law: cart lab (12 minutes)**
1. Students make both predictions, then run Experiment A (vary force at 1 kg) and Experiment B (vary mass at 2 N).
2. Graph a against F, then a against m, then a against 1/m. Ask why the second graph is curved and the third is straight.
3. Point to the m × a column: it matches the net force. Optional: switch to the rough track and ask why the a–F line no longer passes through the origin.

**3. Third law: push off (7 minutes)**
1. Before pushing, students predict which skater feels the bigger force. Try 100 kg against 25 kg.
2. Switch “who pushes” between A, B, and both. Nothing changes. Ask why.
3. Students answer the five partner questions. Spend time on the book on a table: the normal force and weight are equal but are not a pair.

**4. Free-body diagrams (10 minutes)**
1. Do scenario 1 together, then let pairs work through the rest.
2. Stop the class at scenario 2 (crate at steady speed) and scenario 3 (ball tossed up). These are the two that most often reveal the motion-needs-force idea.

**5. Elevator ride (5 minutes)**
1. Students make all three predictions for the upward ride before starting. Then predict the downward ride.
2. Ask: “Why is speeding up going down the same as slowing down going up?” (Both are acceleration downward.)
3. Try the drop tower: the scale reads zero in free fall, though gravity still acts.

**6. Practice (10 minutes, or homework)**
Students complete a round of six. Use “Redo the ones I slipped on” before dealing a new round.

## Discussion prompts
- Why does a hockey puck slide much farther than a book pushed across a table at the same speed?
- When the speed of the block is constant, which is bigger: the push or friction? How do you know?
- In the cart lab, what happens to acceleration when you double the force? Double the mass? How does your graph show it?
- A horse pulls a cart. If the cart pulls back on the horse just as hard, how does anything move? (Look at the forces on the horse alone: the ground pushes it forward harder than the cart pulls it back.)
- A ball is at the very top of its flight. Is the net force on it zero? (No, gravity still acts; its velocity is zero only for an instant and is changing.)
- Why does a scale read 0 N in free fall even though gravity is still pulling on you?

## Misconceptions and how to address them
- **Motion needs a force:** Part 1 frictionless run and steady-speed challenge; free-body “force of motion” distractor; practice problems on the space probe and puck.
- **Net force points along the motion:** Crate at steady speed (zero), ball tossed up (down), sliding puck (backward); the elevator slowing on the way up.
- **A thrown object carries the force of the throw:** “Force from the throw or hit” distractor with an explanation in scenarios 3 and 4.
- **Bigger or pushing object exerts more force:** Part 3 skaters with unequal masses and the “who pushes” switch; collision practice problems detect mass-scaled answers.
- **Third-law pairs cancel / weight and normal force are a pair:** Part 3 takeaway and partner question 4; the partner rule “swap the two objects”.
- **A scale reads your weight:** Part 5 predictions and graph; practice elevator problems detect mg and sign errors.
- **Mass and weight are the same:** Practice weight problems detect answering with the mass and using Earth's g on the Moon.

## Assessment / expected responses
- Strong first-law explanation: “Once you let go nothing pushes it and nothing slows it, so the net force is zero and the velocity stays the same. On a real floor, friction is the force that slows it down.”
- Cart lab claim with evidence: “At 1 kg, 1 N gave about 1 m/s² and 2 N gave about 2 m/s², so doubling the force doubled the acceleration. At 2 N, 1 kg gave about 2 m/s² and 2 kg gave about 1 m/s². The graph of a against 1/m is a straight line with slope about 2 N, the net force.”
- 40 N push, 20 N friction, 5 kg box: a = (40 − 20)/5 = 4 m/s².
- 60 kg student, elevator going up and speeding up at 2 m/s²: N = 60(9.8 + 2) = 708 N. Going up and slowing at 2 m/s²: 60(9.8 − 2) = 468 N.
- 2,000 kg truck, 1,000 kg car, 12,000 N on the car: 12,000 N on the truck; car 12 m/s², truck 6 m/s².

## Accommodations and extension ideas
- Support: Do parts 1 and 4 together on the projector before independent work. Allow calculators. In practice, students can open the worked solution after one miss.
- Support: Pair students so one controls the simulation and the other records predictions and results, then switch.
- Challenge: In the cart lab, use the rough track to measure the friction force from the a–F graph's intercept.
- Challenge: In part 3, compute momentum (m × v) for each skater after the push. What do you notice? (Equal and opposite, so the total stays zero.) This previews conservation of momentum.
- Extension: For the 60 kg rider, find the elevator acceleration at which the scale would read 0 N. What does that mean? (a = −g: free fall.)
- Hands-on follow-up, if the school has the equipment: dynamics carts with force and motion sensors to repeat the cart lab with real data. Follow the school's lab safety rules.

## Simplifications / limitations
- Objects are treated as single rigid bodies; rotation is ignored.
- Friction is one constant value opposing sliding, and the same limit is used for starting and sliding (real starting friction is usually larger). Friction values for wood, carpet, and ice are illustrative.
- The frictionless floor and smooth track are idealizations.
- Air resistance is ignored except for the skydiver.
- Cart-lab readings include simulated ±4% sensor error. The pull is a constant force, not a hanging mass.
- Elevator accelerations of 2 and 3 m/s² exaggerate ordinary elevators; the drop tower is a thought experiment.
- g = 9.8 m/s². Practice answers within 2% are accepted.
- Free-body diagrams draw every force from the object's center. Some courses draw contact forces from the point of contact; tell students which convention your course uses.
