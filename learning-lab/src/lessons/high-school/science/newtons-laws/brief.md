# Lesson brief: Newton's Laws of Motion

- Section / subject / level: High School / Science (physics) / Grades 9 to 12. Fits a first physics course or the forces unit of Physical Science.
- Student learning objective (observable): The student uses Newton's three laws to explain and predict motion. They explain why an object keeps a constant velocity when the net force on it is zero, analyze their own cart-lab data to support the claim that a = F_net / m, identify third-law partner forces and explain why they don't cancel, draw a free-body diagram for an everyday situation and choose the net force direction from the motion, and calculate acceleration, net force, mass, weight, or the reading of a scale in an accelerating elevator.
- Topic / motivating question: What does it take to change how something moves? (Forces change motion; they are not needed to keep it.)
- Standards: None listed yet. Candidate, to be confirmed by the teacher: Oklahoma Academic Standards for Science, Physics, Motion and Stability: Forces and Interactions, performance expectation 1 (Newton's second law from data), Okla. Admin. Code 210:15-3-82(2)(A), amended effective September 11, 2021. See `curriculum/high-school/scope-and-sequence.md`. No code is listed in `lesson.json` `standards` until confirmed.
- Lesson type: Interactive (browser), six parts meant to be done in order:
  1. **First law: keep sliding.** Shove or hold-to-push a 1 kg block on wood, carpet, ice, and an imagined frictionless floor. Arrows show push, friction, and net force; a speed–time graph records each run. Students predict the frictionless case first. Challenge: hold the push at exactly the friction force and watch the speed stay constant.
  2. **Second law: cart lab.** Choose pulling force (0.5 to 3 N), cart mass (0.5 to 2.5 kg), and smooth or rough track. Each run shows a strobe (position every 0.2 s) and records the measured acceleration (with simulated ±4% sensor error) in a data table that includes m × a. Graph a against F, m, or 1/m with a best-fit line, and feedback that flags uncontrolled variables. Two predictions (double the force, double the mass) are checked against the student's own data.
  3. **Third law: push off.** Two skaters of chosen masses push apart on ice; “who pushes” can be A, B, or both, and the forces are the same every time. Then five “find the partner force” questions, including the normal-force-versus-weight trap.
  4. **Free-body diagrams.** Eight scenarios (book, crate at steady speed, ball tossed up, sliding puck, hanging lamp, skydiver at terminal velocity, person in an elevator speeding up, sled pulled by a rope). Students pick the forces from a palette that includes “force of motion” and “force from the throw” distractors, and the net force direction. Feedback names each missing or extra force; the answer is drawn as a scaled free-body diagram.
  5. **Elevator ride.** Stand on a scale in an elevator going up or down (1, 2, or 3 m/s²) or on a drop-tower ride. Students predict the reading for speeding up, steady speed, and slowing down; the ride shows the live reading, a free-body diagram, N = m(g + a), and a reading–time graph.
  6. **Practice.** Rounds of six problems drawn from ten generators (acceleration, net force, mass, push minus friction, constant velocity, elevator scale, weight on Earth or Moon, collision third law, first-law concepts), with feedback aimed at specific slips.
- Target duration: About 50 minutes for all six (one class period). Parts 1 to 3 work as a 25-minute lab; part 4 alone as a 10-minute warm-up; parts 5 and 6 as a review.
- Setting: Individual or paired work on Chromebooks, tablets, or phones; whole-class demonstration on a projector.
- Prior knowledge: speed, velocity, acceleration as change in velocity per second, and solving one-step equations.
- Key misconceptions: see `lesson.json` `misconceptions`. The big four: motion needs a force; the net force points along the motion; the bigger or pushing object exerts more force; a scale always reads your weight.
- Student actions and feedback: predictions before each key simulation, with feedback that cites what the student just saw or measured; hold-to-push control of the block; data collection and graphing; force selection with per-force explanations; staged practice with targeted slip messages and worked solutions.
- Formative assessment / evidence of learning: frictionless prediction; the steady-speed challenge; the cart-lab data table and graph; third-law quiz; free-body diagram scores (first try versus later); elevator predictions; practice round score. Nothing is stored.
- Accessibility / device constraints: All controls are buttons, sliders, checkboxes, or text inputs with labels and work by keyboard (the hold-to-push button responds to the space bar or Enter). Simulations are started by the student and can be paused; the cart lab has an “Animate the run” checkbox that is off by default under reduced motion. Readouts and feedback use polite live regions; every drawing has a text label. Layouts stack at phone width, and drawings redraw with larger labels.
- Reliable educational sources: OpenStax *Physics* (2020), Chapter 4, Sections 4.1 to 4.4 and the chapter's key equations; OpenStax *College Physics 2e* Section 4.2 for the wording of the first law. All drawings are original SVG. No textbook images or problem banks were copied.
- Known simplifications and limitations: see `lesson.json` `modelLimitations`.
- Reviewer sign-off needed: Yes, a high-school physics or physical science teacher. Check the physics, the friction simplification, the free-body diagram conventions (some courses draw forces from the object's edge rather than its center), the elevator values, and the candidate standard.
