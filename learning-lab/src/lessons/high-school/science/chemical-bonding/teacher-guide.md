# Teacher guide: The Periodic Table and Chemical Bonding

**Status:** Demo — not approved for classroom use yet.

## Outcomes
Students read valence electrons for main-group elements from the periodic table; predict and explain which of two atoms is larger, has the higher first ionization energy, or is more electronegative; model ionic bonding as electron transfer to full shells and write binary ionic formulas from ion charges; complete Lewis structures for simple molecules; and classify a bond as nonpolar covalent, polar covalent, or ionic.

The idea the lesson keeps returning to: **how strongly the nucleus pulls on the outer electrons.** More protons pulling on the same shell (across a period) means a stronger pull: smaller atoms, higher ionization energy, higher electronegativity. Another shell farther out (down a group) means a weaker pull. Bonding follows from the same pull: a weak hold gives electrons away (metals), a strong pull takes them (nonmetals), and two strong pulls share.

## Before class
- Work through all six parts once. Parts 1 to 5 are exploration; part 6 is practice.
- In **Read the table**, click Na, then Cl, then Ga, Sn, and a transition metal, to see how the facts panel handles each case.
- In **Spot the patterns**, switch between the three properties before class and look at the right side of periods 4 and 5, where the radius values flatten. Questions 8 and 9 of the prediction round are real exceptions.
- In **Ionic bonds**, build Al₂O₃ yourself: it needs adding atoms on both sides.
- In **Covalent bonds**, solve CO₂ and HCN; the per-atom messages are the main feedback.
- In **Polar or ionic?**, try Li–I and Si–F, where the two rules of thumb disagree.
- On a projector the full 18-column table reads well at full width. On phones the table starts in the "Main groups" view; the full table scrolls sideways inside its own box.
- Nothing is saved, and no student information is collected.

## Facilitation steps
**Read the table (10 minutes)**
1. Ask: "Sodium is a soft metal that reacts violently with water; chlorine is a poisonous gas. Together they make table salt. What changed?" Leave it open.
2. Have students click Li, Na, and K, then across period 3. Ask what stays the same down a column and what changes across a row.
3. Stress that the shell model is a counting tool, not a picture of electron paths.
4. Students do the pattern check until they get three in a row.

**Spot the patterns (10 to 15 minutes)**
1. Color by atomic radius first. Ask students to describe the pattern in words before opening "Why does…".
2. Push on the common misconception: "Chlorine has more electrons than sodium. Why is it smaller?"
3. Run the prediction round in pairs. When the N vs O and Be vs B questions come up, discuss why a trend can be real and still have exceptions.

**Ionic bonds (10 minutes)**
1. Students predict before building. Start with Na + Cl, then Mg + O, then Ca + Cl (the first that needs more atoms).
2. Ask: "Why doesn't chlorine give its electrons to sodium?" Use the ionization energy and electronegativity numbers in the box below the builder.
3. Read "A model, not a movie" together. Make sure students know NaCl is a lattice, not a pair.

**Covalent bonds (10 minutes)**
1. Start with H₂ and H₂O, then CH₄. Point out the pattern C 4, N 3, O 2, H 1.
2. Then O₂, N₂, and CO₂: students should find they need double and triple bonds.
3. Turn on partial charges for H₂O and CO₂ and use the CO₂ caption to raise molecular polarity as a follow-up topic.

**Polar or ionic? (5 to 10 minutes)**
1. Walk the scale from H–H to C–H to H–Cl to Na–Cl.
2. Show Li–I and Si–F. Discuss why chemists treat 0.4 and 1.8 as guides, and how properties (melting point, conductivity when melted) settle real cases.

**Practice (10 minutes)**
Students do a round alone. Afterward, ask which kind of question they missed and have them redo the matching part.

## Discussion prompts
- Why does the number of valence electrons repeat every row, while the number of shells keeps growing?
- Chlorine has more electrons than sodium. Why is it the smaller atom?
- Why do metals end up as positive ions and nonmetals as negative ions? Use ionization energy and electronegativity in your answer.
- Explain why the formula of aluminum oxide is Al₂O₃ without using a "criss-cross" rule.
- Why does nitrogen form a triple bond in N₂ but only single bonds in NH₃?
- CO₂ has two polar bonds. Why is the molecule nonpolar?
- Is the line between "polar covalent" and "ionic" a real line in nature? What evidence would you use to decide for a particular compound?

## Misconceptions and how to address them
- **More electrons means a bigger atom:** Part 2's first question (Na vs Cl) targets this; the explanation says the outer shell is the same and Cl has 6 more protons pulling on it.
- **Valence electrons = period number, or group number 13 to 18 counted as 3 to 8 wrong:** The pattern check (part 1) and practice detect both answers and explain them.
- **Metals gain electrons:** The ionic builder only lets metals give; practice says "right size, wrong sign" when the sign is flipped.
- **1:1 or swapped formulas:** The formula prediction (part 3) and practice include these distractors and explain charge balance.
- **Ionic compounds are molecules:** "A model, not a movie" in part 3; the success message says the formula is a ratio.
- **Bonds are either ionic or covalent:** Part 5's continuous scale and the disagreeing examples.
- **Trends have no exceptions:** The two exception questions in part 2, and the flat radius values in periods 4 and 5.
- **Electrons orbit in circles:** The shell model caption.

## Assessment / expected responses
- Valence electrons of selenium: 6 (group 16 → 16 − 10).
- Larger atom, Mg or S: Mg. Same shell, but S has 4 more protons pulling it in (Mg 141 pm, S 105 pm).
- Higher first ionization energy, Li or K: Li. K's outer electron is one shell farther out and more shielded (Li 520, K 419 kJ/mol).
- Formula of calcium nitride: Ca²⁺ and N³⁻; 3 × (+2) + 2 × (−3) = 0, so Ca₃N₂.
- Lewis structure of HCN: H–C≡N with one lone pair on N.
- Bond type for H–Cl (3.16 − 2.20 = 0.96): polar covalent, with δ− on Cl.
- Strong explanation of bond type: "Na holds its one outer electron loosely and Cl pulls hard (electronegativities 0.93 and 3.16), so the electron transfers completely, and the Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions attract."

## Accommodations and extension ideas
- Support: Use the "Main groups" view throughout, and keep part 3 to Na + Cl, Mg + O, and Ca + Cl.
- Support: Pair students so one predicts and one operates the builder, then switch.
- Support: Everything works by keyboard; the table moves with arrow keys.
- Challenge: Predict the formula of strontium phosphide and aluminum sulfide without the builder, then explain why Al₂S₃ needs 2 and 3.
- Challenge: Use the radius data to explain why the trend is nearly flat from Ga to Br.
- Extension: Draw the Lewis structure of CO (it needs formal charges, which this builder does not handle) and compare it with CO₂.
- Extension: Research why O₂ is attracted to a magnet even though its Lewis structure shows all electrons paired.

## Simplifications / limitations
- The shell model is a counting diagram, labeled as such.
- Electron counting, ion predictions, and data cover the main-group elements of periods 1 to 5 only.
- Atomic radius is the single-bond covalent radius; noble gases are left blank.
- The ionic builder treats electron transfer one electron at a time as bookkeeping; real ionic solids are lattices.
- The Lewis builder covers neutral molecules without formal charges and assumes the octet rule.
- The bond-type cutoffs are a common textbook guide, not a law; other books use slightly different numbers.
- Practice questions are generated from templates and avoid borderline pairs.
