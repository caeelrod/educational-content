# Teacher guide: Inside a Human Cell

**Status:** Demo — not approved for classroom use yet.

## Outcomes
Students identify the main parts of a typical human cell and describe what each does. They trace how a cell builds a protein and ships it out (nucleus → ribosome → rough ER → Golgi → cell membrane, with vesicles carrying it between stops and mitochondria supplying energy). They explain how a specialized cell's shape and parts fit its job.

## Before class
- Open **Explore** and try all three ways of choosing a part: point with a mouse (the zoom view follows after a short pause), tap on a touchscreen (tap the same part again to look closer), and keyboard (click or Tab to the cell, then arrow keys, Enter, and Escape).
- Decide whether to use **City jobs**. Analogies help many students, but plan to ask where each one breaks down.
- Check the drawing from the back of the room on your projector. **Show labels** adds labels to the whole cell.
- Nothing is saved, and no student information is collected. Scores disappear when the page closes.

## Facilitation steps
**Whole-class tour (10 minutes)**
1. Start in **Explore** with nothing selected. Ask: "What do you think is inside a cell?" Then reveal parts one at a time from the part buttons.
2. For the nucleus, mitochondria, and membrane, press **Look closer** and have students choose numbered spots.
3. Open **Looking for a cell wall or chloroplasts?** to contrast with plant cells.

**Protein journey (10 minutes)**
1. Step through the seven stops. Pause on each **Discuss** prompt for a turn-and-talk.
2. On the ★ step, have students order the stops without looking back. Wrong taps get a hint about where the protein goes next.

**Find the part (5 to 10 minutes)**
1. Start with **Its name**, then move to **What it does** and **Its city job**.
2. On a whiteboard, students can take turns tapping. Individually, have them replay **Practice the ones I missed**.

**Cell gallery (5 to 10 minutes)**
1. Assign each group one cell. Groups answer the **Think about it** prompt and use the link to find the related part in Explore.
2. Share out: "How does your cell's shape help it do its job?"

## Discussion prompts
- Why might the cell keep its DNA inside the nucleus and only send out copies?
- A muscle cell needs a lot of energy. Which part would it have lots of?
- A red blood cell has no nucleus. What can't it do? Why might it wear out after about four months?
- How is the Golgi like a post office? Where does that comparison stop working?
- Why can't a human cell have a cell wall and still change shape or crawl?

## Misconceptions and how to address them
- **Cells are empty bags:** The cytoplasm description and the crowded drawing show that a cell is packed and busy. Remind students that real cells are even more crowded.
- **All cells look alike:** The gallery shows six very different cells, and its intro says the Explore cell is a "typical" model.
- **One nucleus each:** The nucleus "How many?" card and the gallery (red blood cells: none; skeletal muscle: many) address this directly.
- **Mitochondria make energy:** The text says they *release* energy stored in food, and the power-plant analogy explains that a power plant doesn't make energy from nothing either.
- **Cell walls and chloroplasts:** The expandable note in Explore explains these belong to plant cells.
- **DNA leaves the nucleus:** Journey step 2 says only a copy (messenger RNA) leaves; the DNA stays.
- **Colors and sizes are real:** The note under every activity says the colors are added and sizes aren't to scale.
- **Mixing up similar parts:** Find the part has specific hints for nucleus vs. nucleolus, rough vs. smooth ER, ribosome vs. rough ER, vesicle vs. lysosome, and membrane vs. cytoplasm vs. cytoskeleton.

## Assessment / expected responses
- Protein order: nucleus (DNA and the copy), ribosome, rough ER, Golgi apparatus, cell membrane. Strong answers mention vesicles carrying it between stops and mitochondria supplying energy.
- Muscle cells have lots of mitochondria because they use a lot of energy to contract.
- White blood cells need lots of lysosomes to digest the germs they swallow.
- A red blood cell can't make new proteins without a nucleus, so it can't repair itself and wears out.
- Strong explanation of the membrane: "It lets some things in and keeps others out, so the inside of the cell can stay different from the outside."

## Accommodations and extension ideas
- Support: Use **Show labels** and **Its name** clues first. Use the read-aloud button. Limit to five parts (nucleus, membrane, cytoplasm, mitochondria, ribosomes).
- Support: Keyboard and switch users can do everything with Tab, arrow keys, Enter, and Escape.
- Challenge: Use **What it does** clues and the close-up views. Ask students to write a clue for a partner.
- Challenge: Ask students to invent a new analogy (a school, a factory, a sports team) and say where it breaks down.
- Extension: Research a cell type not in the gallery (for example, a cone cell in the eye) and predict which parts it has a lot of.

## Simplifications / limitations
- The cell is a flat, conceptual drawing, not to scale. Ribosomes, pores, and vesicles are drawn much too big, and colors are added.
- Only a few copies of many parts are drawn, and some parts (peroxisomes, intermediate filaments other than keratin) are left out.
- The protein journey shows one protein and skips how its "address" is read.
- Cellular respiration is simplified; the first steps of breaking down sugar happen in the cytoplasm, which the cytoplasm card mentions.
- City analogies are deliberately imperfect, and the lesson asks students where they break down.
- The read-aloud button depends on the device's built-in voices and is hidden when they aren't available.
